About This Collection

For my curated information collection, I am focusing on Digital Forensics and Incident Response (DFIR) resources for people entering the cybersecurity field. This collection includes resources that explain how cybersecurity incidents are investigated, how digital evidence is identified and preserved, and how investigators use different tools and techniques to determine what happened during an incident. I chose this topic because I am working toward transitioning into cybersecurity and have become especially interested in the investigative side of the field.

The collection focuses primarily on beginner and early-intermediate DFIR resources. It includes incident response procedures, digital evidence handling, Windows and network forensics, threat analysis, forensic tools, and hands-on practice environments. I have avoided highly specialized areas such as advanced malware reverse engineering and expert-level mobile device forensics because I wanted the collection to remain useful to someone who is still developing foundational skills.

I organized the collection into five categories: DFIR Foundations, Incident Response, Digital Forensics Tools and Techniques, Threat Analysis and Investigation, and Hands-On Practice. The categories are intended to move a learner from understanding basic concepts and investigative procedures into using tools and working with actual evidence. My goal is to provide a practical starting point that helps someone understand not only what DFIR is, but how those concepts are applied during an investigation.

Organizational Structure

I organized my collection into five main categories. This structure is designed to guide someone from understanding the basic concepts of DFIR into more practical investigative skills and hands-on experience.

DFIR Foundations

Introductory resources covering digital forensics, terminology, evidence handling, and investigative processes.

Incident Response

Resources covering preparation, detection, containment, eradication, recovery, and documentation during cybersecurity incidents.

Digital Forensics Tools and Techniques

Resources focused on locating, collecting, preserving, and analyzing digital evidence from computers and other systems.

Threat Analysis and Investigation

Resources that help connect forensic evidence to attacker behavior, tactics, techniques, and investigative findings.

Hands-On Practice

Labs and exercises that allow users to practice digital investigations using realistic evidence and cybersecurity scenarios.

I chose this structure because someone entering DFIR needs more than a collection of tools. They first need to understand the investigation process, learn how evidence is collected and analyzed, and then practice those skills. These categories also allow the collection to expand as I add more advanced resources later.

DFIR Foundations

NIST SP 800-86 – Guide to Integrating Forensic Techniques into Incident Response

Category: DFIR Foundations > Forensic Process

Source Type: Government Publication

Kent, K., Chevalier, S., Grance, T., & Dang, H. (2006). Guide to Integrating Forensic Techniques into Incident Response. NIST Special Publication 800-86. National Institute of Standards and Technology.

What it covers: NIST SP 800-86 explains how computer and network forensic techniques can be incorporated into cybersecurity incident response. It describes the forensic process of collecting, examining, analyzing, and reporting digital evidence and discusses evidence from files, operating systems, applications, and network traffic.

Why it's valuable: This publication provides a strong foundation for understanding how forensic analysis supports an investigation. It is especially useful because it emphasizes preserving evidence integrity, documenting investigative actions, and connecting technical findings to the larger incident response process.

Who would benefit: Students, beginning DFIR professionals, incident responders, and anyone learning how forensic evidence is collected and analyzed would benefit from this resource.

Limitations: The publication was released in 2006, so some technology-specific examples and tools are dated. It should be paired with newer resources and hands-on training, but the investigative principles and forensic process remain useful for understanding DFIR fundamentals.

13Cubed – Introduction to Windows Forensics

Category: DFIR Foundations > Windows Forensics

Source Type: Video

13Cubed. (2017, June 4). Introduction to Windows Forensics. YouTube.

What it covers: 13Cubed's Introduction to Windows Forensics provides a beginner-friendly walkthrough of common Windows artifacts used during digital investigations. The video demonstrates artifacts such as UserAssist, ShellBags, USB device history, network information, LNK files, and Prefetch while examining a Windows 10 forensic image.

Why it's valuable: It is valuable because it moves beyond definitions and shows what forensic evidence actually looks like on a system. This helps connect the investigative process described in formal sources such as NIST SP 800-86 to practical examination of evidence.

Who would benefit: Students and people entering DFIR would benefit most because the explanations are visual and do not assume advanced forensic experience.

Limitations: The video was published in 2017, so some Windows-specific details and the original cheat sheet are older. However, the core artifacts and investigative concepts remain useful, and newer reference material can be used alongside the video.

Incident Response

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 – Incident Response Recommendations and Considerations for Cybersecurity Risk Management

Category: Incident Response > Frameworks and Processes

Source Type: Government Publication

Nelson, A., Rekhi, S., Scarfone, K., & Souppaya, M. (2025). Incident Response Recommendations and Considerations for Cybersecurity Risk Management: A CSF 2.0 Community Profile. NIST Special Publication 800-61 Rev. 3.

What it covers: NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 explains how organizations can incorporate incident response into broader cybersecurity risk management. It connects preparation, detection, response, and recovery activities to the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 and emphasizes that incident response should be part of normal organizational risk management.

Why it's valuable: The publication provides current federal guidance and shows how professional organizations plan for, coordinate, and improve their response to cybersecurity incidents. It also helps connect technical incident response activities to organizational decision-making, communication, recovery, and long-term security improvement.

Who would benefit: Students, entry-level cybersecurity professionals, SOC analysts, incident responders, and organizations developing formal response procedures would benefit from this resource.

Limitations: The publication focuses more on organizational strategy, planning, and risk management than hands-on forensic investigation. Readers will need additional labs and technical resources to practice specific DFIR tools, evidence analysis, and investigative techniques.

NIST SP 800-184 – Guide for Cybersecurity Event Recovery

Category: Incident Response > Recovery and Resilience

Source Type: Government Publication

Bartock, M., Cichonski, J., Souppaya, M., Smith, M., Witte, G., & Scarfone, K. (2016). Guide for Cybersecurity Event Recovery. NIST Special Publication 800-184.

What it covers: NIST SP 800-184 focuses on what organizations should do to prepare for and recover from cybersecurity incidents. It discusses recovery planning, development of recovery playbooks, testing, improvement, and the use of lessons learned from previous events.

Why it's valuable: Incident response does not end when malicious activity is contained. Restoring systems, maintaining business operations, and improving future resilience are also part of an effective response program. The guide complements NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 in this collection by providing additional detail on the recovery side of incident management.

Who would benefit: Students, incident responders, security managers, and organizations developing response procedures would benefit from it.

Limitations: The publication dates to 2016, so some organizational examples and terminology may not reflect the newest cybersecurity practices. Its planning and recovery principles, however, remain broadly applicable.

FIRST – CSIRT Services Framework

Category: Incident Response > Teams and Professional Services

Source Type: Professional Framework

Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams (FIRST). Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT) Services Framework, Version 2.1.

What it covers: The FIRST CSIRT Services Framework describes the services and functions that Computer Security Incident Response Teams may provide. Why it's valuable: The framework shows that incident response is not only a sequence of technical actions; it is also a professional service that requires defined responsibilities, communication, coordination, and repeatable processes. Who would benefit: Students, people preparing for SOC or incident-response careers, and organizations building or improving a CSIRT would benefit from this source.

Limitations: The framework is designed at a high organizational level and does not teach specific forensic tools or investigative techniques. Beginners may need practical labs and technical resources to see how these services are carried out during an actual investigation.

Digital Forensics Tools and Techniques

SWGDE – Best Practices for Computer Forensic Acquisition

Category: Digital Forensics Tools and Techniques > Evidence Acquisition

Scientific Working Group on Digital Evidence (SWGDE). Best Practices for Computer Forensic Acquisition. SWGDE Publication 17-F-002.

What it covers: This SWGDE document describes best practices for acquiring digital evidence from computers and storage media while protecting the integrity of the evidence. It addresses acquisition methods, documentation, and situations where normal procedures may need to be modified.

Why it's valuable: Evidence acquisition is a fundamental DFIR skill, and SWGDE guidance is developed through a professional consensus process involving digital forensic subject matter experts.

Who would benefit: Students moving from basic forensic concepts into evidence collection and practitioners who want a professional reference.

Limitations: The document is not intended as a training manual. Beginners may need additional instruction to understand some terminology and apply the procedures correctly.

Autopsy – Digital Forensics Platform

Category: Digital Forensics Tools and Techniques > Disk and File System Analysis

Source Type: Digital Forensics Tool

Sleuth Kit Labs. Autopsy Digital Forensics Platform.

What it covers: Autopsy is an open-source digital forensics platform built around The Sleuth Kit and other forensic tools. Investigators can use it to examine disk images, hard drives, logical files, and other data sources while searching for evidence such as deleted files, web activity, user files, timelines, and other system artifacts.

Why it's valuable: Autopsy is valuable for beginners because it provides a graphical interface instead of requiring every forensic task to be completed from the command line. It gives learners experience creating cases, adding evidence sources, examining artifacts, and organizing findings in a way that resembles a real forensic investigation.

Who would benefit: Students, new digital forensic investigators, law enforcement personnel, and cybersecurity professionals learning disk and file system analysis would benefit from using Autopsy.

Limitations: Although the interface makes forensic analysis easier to approach, users still need to understand the artifacts they are examining. Processing large forensic images can also require significant storage, memory, and processing time.

Volatility 3 – Volatile Memory Analysis Framework

Category: Digital Forensics Tools and Techniques > Memory Forensics

Source Type: Open-Source Forensics Tool

Volatility Foundation. Volatility 3: The Volatile Memory Extraction Framework.

What it covers: Volatility 3 is a digital forensics framework used to extract and analyze artifacts from volatile memory, or RAM. Investigators can examine memory images for information about running processes, network activity, loaded modules, system information, and other evidence that may only exist while a computer is operating.

Why it's valuable: Memory forensics provides a different view of an incident than disk analysis alone. Information found in RAM can help investigators identify malware, suspicious processes, network connections, and other activity that may not be obvious from files stored on a drive. Volatility is widely used within the DFIR community and provides a practical way to learn this type of analysis.

Who would benefit: Students who already understand basic forensic concepts and want to move into memory analysis, malware investigation, or incident response would benefit most from this resource.

Limitations: Volatility does not acquire memory itself, so investigators need a memory image collected with another tool. It is also command-line based and requires more technical knowledge than graphical tools, which may make it challenging for complete beginners.

KAPE – Kroll Artifact Parser and Extractor

Category: Digital Forensics Tools and Techniques > Artifact Collection and Triage

Source Type: Forensic Collection and Processing Tool

Kroll. Kroll Artifact Parser and Extractor (KAPE).

What it covers: KAPE is a forensic triage tool designed to quickly locate, collect, and process artifacts that may be important during a digital investigation. It uses configurable targets to identify files and artifacts and modules to process the collected information using forensic utilities.

Why it's valuable: KAPE is valuable because investigators do not always have time to wait for a complete forensic image before beginning analysis. It can collect important artifacts quickly and help investigators identify which systems or evidence sources deserve additional attention. This makes it useful for incident response situations where speed can be important.

Who would benefit: Students learning Windows forensics, incident responders, digital forensic investigators, and analysts who need to understand forensic triage would benefit from learning KAPE.

Limitations: KAPE requires an understanding of the artifacts being collected and how its targets and modules work. Its licensing also varies by use. Kroll allows educational, research, government, and internal company use, but commercial use on third-party networks or as part of a paid engagement is restricted.

Wireshark – Network Protocol Analyzer

Category: Digital Forensics Tools and Techniques > Network Forensics

Source Type: Network Analysis Tool

Wireshark Foundation. Wireshark Network Protocol Analyzer.

What it covers: Wireshark is an open-source network protocol analyzer that allows investigators to capture live network traffic or examine previously collected packet capture files. It provides detailed information about network protocols, IP addresses, communication sessions, packet contents, and other activity moving across a network.

Why it's valuable: Network traffic can provide important evidence during an incident, especially when investigators need to determine which systems communicated, what protocols were used, or whether suspicious connections occurred. Wireshark allows learners to see network activity at the packet level and connects networking knowledge directly to incident investigation.

Who would benefit: Students with basic networking knowledge, SOC analysts, incident responders, and investigators learning network forensics would benefit from this tool.

Limitations: Wireshark can be overwhelming when analyzing large packet captures, and understanding the results requires knowledge of networking and common protocols. Encryption can also prevent investigators from viewing the actual contents of some communications even when the traffic itself is visible.

Threat Analysis and Investigation

MITRE – Getting Started with ATT&CK

Category: Threat Analysis and Investigation > Adversary Behavior

Applebaum, A., Nickels, K., Schulz, T., Strom, B., Wunder, J., & Pennington, A. (2019). Getting Started with ATT&CK. MITRE.

What it covers: MITRE's Getting Started with ATT&CK introduces the ATT&CK knowledge base and explains how cybersecurity professionals can use documented adversary tactics and techniques to support threat-informed defense. It focuses on practical ways organizations can begin using ATT&CK.

Why it's valuable: The guide helps connect evidence found during an investigation to known attacker behaviors, giving investigators a common way to describe what an adversary may have done.

Who would benefit: Students and early-career cybersecurity professionals moving from basic digital forensics into threat analysis and incident investigation.

Limitations: The guide was published in 2019, and ATT&CK continues to evolve. The framework can also be overwhelming for readers without basic cybersecurity terminology.

Sigma – Generic Detection Rule Format

Category: Threat Analysis and Investigation > Detection Engineering

Source Type: Open-Source Detection Standard and Rule Collection

SigmaHQ. Sigma: Generic Signature Format for SIEM Systems.

What it covers: Sigma is an open detection-rule format used to describe suspicious activity found in log data. Rules identify specific patterns, behaviors, log sources, and conditions that may indicate malicious activity. The Sigma project also maintains a large community collection of detection rules that can be adapted for different security platforms and SIEM systems.

Why it's valuable: Sigma helps connect threat behavior to actual security monitoring. Instead of focusing only on indicators such as IP addresses or file hashes, analysts can create detections based on attacker behavior. This complements MITRE ATT&CK because ATT&CK describes how adversaries operate, while Sigma provides a practical way to detect some of those behaviors in security logs.

Who would benefit: SOC analysts, threat hunters, incident responders, detection engineers, and students learning how security monitoring supports investigations would benefit from Sigma.

Limitations: Sigma rules depend on the quality and availability of log data. Rules may also require modification for a specific environment or SIEM platform and can produce false positives if they are used without testing and tuning.

MISP – Open Source Threat Intelligence Platform

Category: Threat Analysis and Investigation > Threat Intelligence Sharing

Source Type: Open-Source Threat Intelligence Platform

MISP Project. MISP Open Source Threat Intelligence and Sharing Platform.

What it covers: MISP is an open-source platform for collecting, storing, analyzing, correlating, and sharing threat intelligence. Analysts can use it to organize indicators of compromise, malware information, threat actor data, attack techniques, and other intelligence connected to cybersecurity incidents. MISP also supports taxonomies, feeds, automation, APIs, and integration with other security tools.

Why it's valuable: MISP demonstrates how information discovered during one investigation can become useful intelligence for future investigations. Indicators and related information can be shared between analysts and organizations, allowing teams to identify relationships between attacks, campaigns, malware, and threat actors. It also works well alongside MITRE ATT&CK by helping analysts organize intelligence about adversary behavior.

Who would benefit: Threat intelligence analysts, SOC teams, incident responders, investigators, and students learning how intelligence supports cybersecurity investigations would benefit from MISP.

Limitations: MISP can be complex for beginners because effective use requires understanding indicators, threat intelligence, sharing models, tagging, and correlation. The quality of the intelligence also depends on the accuracy and reliability of the data being entered or received from other sources.

Tool Selection

For this project, I chose GitHub Pages to build and publish my curated DFIR collection. GitHub Pages allowed me to organize the collection into clear sections while giving me control over the design, navigation, and overall presentation of the site. I was able to create separate categories for DFIR foundations, incident response, forensic tools and techniques, threat analysis, and hands-on practice while keeping the resources easy to navigate.

GitHub Pages also fits well with the technical nature of this project. Building the collection gave me additional practice with HTML, CSS, Git, and GitHub while creating a resource that can continue to grow after the class is complete. The site can be updated as I find new DFIR resources, tools, and training opportunities without rebuilding the entire project. Using GitHub Pages also makes the collection publicly accessible through a web browser, which allows it to function as both a class project and a resource I could share with someone interested in learning more about Digital Forensics and Incident Response.
