About This Collection

For my curated information collection, I am focusing on Digital Forensics and Incident Response (DFIR) resources for people entering the cybersecurity field. The collection will include resources that explain how cybersecurity incidents are investigated, how digital evidence is identified and preserved, and how investigators use different tools and techniques to determine what happened during an incident. I chose this topic because I am working toward transitioning into cybersecurity and have become especially interested in the investigative side of the field.

The scope of my collection will focus primarily on beginner and early-intermediate DFIR resources. I will include incident response procedures, digital evidence handling, Windows and network forensics, threat analysis, forensic tools, and hands-on practice environments. I will exclude highly specialized areas such as advanced malware reverse engineering, expert-level mobile device forensics, and commercial forensic products that are not practical for someone beginning to learn the field. The goal is to create a collection that gives someone a clear path from understanding DFIR concepts to actually practicing them.

Organizational Structure

I plan to organize my collection into five main categories. This structure is designed to guide someone from understanding the basic concepts of DFIR into more practical investigative skills and hands-on experience.

DFIR Foundations

Introductory resources covering digital forensics, terminology, evidence handling, and investigative processes.

Incident Response

Resources covering preparation, detection, containment, eradication, recovery, and documentation during cybersecurity incidents.

Digital Forensics Tools and Techniques

Resources focused on locating, collecting, preserving, and analyzing digital evidence from computers and other systems.

Threat Analysis and Investigation

Resources that help connect forensic evidence to attacker behavior, tactics, techniques, and investigative findings.

Hands-On Practice

Labs and exercises that allow users to practice digital investigations using realistic evidence and cybersecurity scenarios.

I chose this structure because someone entering DFIR needs more than a collection of tools. They first need to understand the investigation process, learn how evidence is collected and analyzed, and then practice those skills. These categories also allow the collection to expand as I add more advanced resources later.

Initial Sources

DFIR Foundations

NIST SP 800-86 – Guide to Integrating Forensic Techniques into Incident Response

Category: DFIR Foundations > Forensic Process

Kent, K., Chevalier, S., Grance, T., & Dang, H. (2006). Guide to Integrating Forensic Techniques into Incident Response. NIST Special Publication 800-86. National Institute of Standards and Technology.

What it covers: NIST SP 800-86 explains how computer and network forensic techniques can support cybersecurity incident response. It describes the forensic process of collecting, examining, analyzing, and reporting digital evidence and discusses evidence from files, operating systems, network traffic, and applications.

Why it's valuable: NIST provides an authoritative foundation for understanding how forensic work fits into an investigation.

Who would benefit: Students and beginning DFIR professionals who need a clear introduction to forensic processes and evidence sources.

Limitations: The publication was released in 2006, so some technology-specific guidance is dated. It should be paired with newer resources and hands-on training.

Incident Response

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 – Incident Response Recommendations and Considerations for Cybersecurity Risk Management

Category: Incident Response > Frameworks and Processes

Nelson, A., Rekhi, S., Scarfone, K., & Souppaya, M. (2025). Incident Response Recommendations and Considerations for Cybersecurity Risk Management: A CSF 2.0 Community Profile. NIST Special Publication 800-61 Rev. 3.

What it covers: NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 explains how organizations can incorporate incident response into broader cybersecurity risk management. It connects preparation, detection, response, and recovery activities to the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0.

Why it's valuable: This is current federal guidance and provides a strong framework for understanding how professional organizations plan for and manage cybersecurity incidents.

Who would benefit: Students, entry-level cybersecurity professionals, and anyone learning how incident response fits into an organization's overall security program.

Limitations: The publication focuses more on organizational strategy and risk management than technical investigation. Readers will need additional labs or technical resources to practice specific DFIR tools and procedures.

CISA – Federal Government Cybersecurity Incident and Vulnerability Response Playbooks

Category: Incident Response > Procedures and Playbooks

What it covers: CISA's Cybersecurity Incident and Vulnerability Response Playbooks provide structured procedures for identifying, coordinating, investigating, containing, remediating, recovering from, and documenting cybersecurity incidents and vulnerabilities.

Why it's valuable: The playbooks show how incident response is handled in a structured professional environment and help connect general incident response concepts to actual procedures.

Who would benefit: Students and entry-level cybersecurity professionals who want to understand how incident response teams organize and document their work.

Limitations: The playbooks were written primarily for federal agencies, so some procedures may be more formal than what would be used in smaller organizations.

Digital Forensics Tools and Techniques

SWGDE – Best Practices for Computer Forensic Acquisition

Category: Digital Forensics Tools and Techniques > Evidence Acquisition

Scientific Working Group on Digital Evidence (SWGDE). Best Practices for Computer Forensic Acquisition. SWGDE Publication 17-F-002.

What it covers: This SWGDE document describes best practices for acquiring digital evidence from computers and storage media while protecting the integrity of the evidence. It addresses acquisition methods, documentation, and situations where normal procedures may need to be modified.

Why it's valuable: Evidence acquisition is a fundamental DFIR skill, and SWGDE guidance is developed through a professional consensus process involving digital forensic subject matter experts.

Who would benefit: Students moving from basic forensic concepts into evidence collection and practitioners who want a professional reference.

Limitations: The document is not intended as a training manual. Beginners may need additional instruction to understand some terminology and apply the procedures correctly.

Threat Analysis and Investigation

MITRE – Getting Started with ATT&CK

Category: Threat Analysis and Investigation > Adversary Behavior

Applebaum, A., Nickels, K., Schulz, T., Strom, B., Wunder, J., & Pennington, A. (2019). Getting Started with ATT&CK. MITRE.

What it covers: MITRE's Getting Started with ATT&CK introduces the ATT&CK knowledge base and explains how cybersecurity professionals can use documented adversary tactics and techniques to support threat-informed defense. It focuses on practical ways organizations can begin using ATT&CK.

Why it's valuable: The guide helps connect evidence found during an investigation to known attacker behaviors, giving investigators a common way to describe what an adversary may have done.

Who would benefit: Students and early-career cybersecurity professionals moving from basic digital forensics into threat analysis and incident investigation.

Limitations: The guide was published in 2019, and ATT&CK continues to evolve. The framework can also be overwhelming for readers without basic cybersecurity terminology.

Hands-On Practice

NIST CFReDS – Hacking Case

Category: Hands-On Practice > Disk Forensics

National Institute of Standards and Technology. Hacking Case. Computer Forensic Reference Data Sets (CFReDS).

What it covers: NIST's CFReDS Hacking Case provides a simulated digital forensic investigation using a forensic disk image containing artifacts connected to suspicious computer activity. Learners examine the evidence and attempt to reconstruct what happened on the system.

Why it's valuable: Instead of only reading about forensic procedures, students can work with an actual evidence image and practice identifying and interpreting digital artifacts.

Who would benefit: Students who understand basic forensic concepts and are ready to gain hands-on experience with disk analysis.

Limitations: The scenario is based on older computer technology and requires forensic analysis software, storage space, and some knowledge of working with forensic images. Answers to the case are also available online.

View Resource

Malware-Traffic-Analysis.net – First to Last Traffic Analysis Exercise

Category: Hands-On Practice > Network Forensics

Malware-Traffic-Analysis.net. (2026, August 9). Traffic Analysis Exercise: First to Last.

What it covers: First to Last is a 2026 network traffic analysis exercise built around a simulated Security Operations Center incident. Learners receive malware alerts and a packet capture, then investigate the traffic to identify the infected Windows system and related host and user information.

Why it's valuable: The exercise provides realistic, current practice using packet-capture evidence and requires the learner to produce information that could appear in an incident report.

Who would benefit: Students with basic networking and Wireshark knowledge who want practical experience investigating suspicious network activity.

Limitations: The exercise assumes familiarity with packet analysis and requires software capable of reading PCAP files. The downloadable archive is password-protected, which adds an extra setup step.

Tool Selection

For this project, I chose GitHub Pages to build and publish my curated DFIR collection. GitHub Pages allows me to organize the collection into clear sections while giving me control over the design and navigation of the site. I can create categories for DFIR foundations, incident response, forensic tools and techniques, threat analysis, and hands-on practice while keeping the resources easy to navigate.

GitHub Pages also fits well with the technical nature of this project. Building the collection gives me additional practice with HTML, CSS, Git, and GitHub while creating something that can continue to grow after the class is complete. As I expand the collection from these initial sources to 15–20 resources, I can add new categories, pages, and features without rebuilding the entire project.
